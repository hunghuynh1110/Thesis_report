\section{Answering the research questions}\label{sec:disc-rq}

RQ1 asked for the intrinsic baseline attitude of a model evaluated in a
vacuum, and the chapters above answer that the question presupposes something
the measurement does not support. An intrinsic attitude, as the proposal
conceived it, is a single signed number. What the five models produce is
three quantities that move independently: how often the model assents, how
sharply it distinguishes the two kinds of inventory item, and how much of its
probability lands on the response set at all. Llama-90B scores highest on the
first, lowest on the second among the models that separate at all, and worst
on the third whenever an image is present. Collapsing those into one index
produces $+0.153$, and \cref{sec:s1-aggregate} showed that almost all of it
is the first quantity.

Read as that triple rather than as an index, the answer to RQ1 is consistent
across all five models. None leans towards the attitudes the inventory
measures at the level of individual items: polarity separation is negative
throughout, and where it is large enough to distinguish from chance it is
the two larger Qwen models that reach significance. What differs between
models is not the direction of the item-level pattern but how much response
style sits on top of it, and how much of the behaviour the probe can see.

RQ2 asked whether that intrinsic skew is descriptively consistent with
downstream behaviour, and the honest answer is that the two do not line up.
Ranked by intrinsic score the order is Llama-90B, Llama-11B, Qwen-2B, Qwen-7B
and Qwen-72B; ranked by signed deviation from the human baseline it runs the
other way, at $\rho=-0.70$ with $p=0.188$ over five points that are not
independent. The direction is suggestive and the evidence will not carry it.

What does hold between the two phases is not a correlation but a shared
mechanism. The models whose Phase~1 captured mass collapses under an image
are the Llama pair, and the models whose Phase~2 robustness collapses under
an image are the two largest, Llama-90B among them. In both phases the
presence of an image changes how the model engages with the prompt rather
than only what it answers, which is a stronger and more specific claim than
the one RQ2 was framed to test.

\section{What logit-level auditing reveals}\label{sec:disc-logit}

The clearest benefit of reading the distribution rather than the text is that
a refusal becomes a measurement instead of an obstacle. An audit that samples
tokens and parses the result sees ``I cannot determine that from this image''
as an unparseable string, and either discards the row or counts it as missing.
Reading the distribution sees the same event as captured mass falling from
$0.979$ to $0.237$, which is a number that can be averaged, compared across
conditions and tested. Arditi et al. \cite{arditi2024refusal} showed that
refusal is mediated by a single direction in the residual stream, so it is a
quantity the model carries rather than a failure of the probe, and
\cref{sec:s1-quality} measures exactly that.

The same reading separates two things a text-level audit must conflate. A
model that declines and a model that answers neutrally both produce
uninformative text, but they are far apart in the distribution: the first
moves its mass off the response set while the second splits it evenly within
the set. This is the distinction that makes \cref{tab:capm} interpretable,
and it is why defensiveness could be located in one model family rather than
inferred from the tone of generated sentences.

Phase~2 shows something a text-level audit would also miss, which is that the
image changes how the model uses the prompt and not only what it concludes.
\cref{sec:s2-anchor} found the two largest models applying the stated scale
anchors with no image present and ceasing to apply them reliably when one is
added. The prompt is unchanged, the scale definition is unchanged, and what
degrades is the model's use of an instruction it had been following. That is
a form of visual dominance over the text channel consistent with the mediation
result of Weng et al. \cite{weng2024images}, observed here on a generative
model and through behaviour rather than through component ablation.

The limits of the method deserve the same prominence as its benefits. Wang et
al. \cite{wang2024myanswer} found first token probabilities disagreeing with
generated answers on more than 60\% of multiple choice items, worst in models
heavily tuned on conversational and safety data, which is precisely the class
of model audited here. Every claim in this thesis is therefore a claim about
the next token distribution and not about what a deployed system would print.
Hu and Levy \cite{hu2023prompting} argue that probabilities are the more
faithful readout of what a model has learnt, and both things can hold at once:
the distribution is the better measure of disposition, and the text is what a
user actually receives.

The method also inherits a boundary from the interface rather than from the
model. Captured mass requires the full vocabulary in the denominator, which
closed APIs do not expose, so GPT-4o could not be audited here at all
(\cref{sec:design-models}). An audit designed around open weights is not a
general audit of deployed systems, and the models most widely used as judges
are the ones this method currently cannot reach.

\section{Methodological lessons}\label{sec:disc-lessons}

Three lessons from this project generalise beyond it, and the first is that a
logit-level audit must report its own coverage. Six measurement faults were
found during the work (\cref{tab:faults}), and not one of them raised an
error or produced an aggregate that looked wrong. A probe reading only
lowercase tokens returned a plausible mean $\pyes$ while capturing $0.09\%$
of the distribution, and a model whose hidden state had been zeroed by a
hardware fault returned a mean expected rating of exactly $4.000$, the
midpoint of the scale and a value a calibrated model might well produce. What
exposed them was captured mass and output diversity, neither of which is part
of any standard reporting practice. An audit that reports a probability
without reporting how much of the distribution that probability describes
cannot distinguish a measurement from an artefact.

The second lesson concerns borrowing instruments. A psychometric scale built
for people assumes a respondent whose tendency to agree is a modest
perturbation rather than a free parameter, and the ASI's sixteen to six
keying ratio is safe only under that assumption. A model violates it: mean
$\pyes$ across the five audited here runs from $0.199$ to $0.599$ on the
positively keyed rows alone, which is enough for the keying ratio by itself
to order the models. Tjuatja et al. \cite{tjuatja2024response} and
Dominguez-Olmedo et al. \cite{dominguez2024questioning} report the same
divergence from human response behaviour on other instruments. Scores that are
invariant to a uniform shift in assent, and inference that permutes the
keying rather than assuming a null, are therefore requirements of
administering such a scale to a model rather than refinements of it.

The third lesson is about infrastructure, and it cost this project the most
time. Every small model ran on one GPU and every large model on three, so
model size and GPU count were perfectly confounded across the entire run
history. When the 90B produced degenerate output, the observation that the
same code worked on the 11B appeared to exonerate the code and implicate the
checkpoint, and it did neither: forcing the 11B across three GPUs reproduced
the failure exactly on weights known to be sound. An audit running on shared
cluster hardware should vary the execution path deliberately rather than
letting it track the variable under study, and \cref{sec:s1-replication}
reports the checks that were added once this was understood.
