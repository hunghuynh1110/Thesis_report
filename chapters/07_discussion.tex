\section{Answering the research questions}\label{sec:disc-rq}

The results address two questions: how the models respond to the inventory,
and whether those responses correspond with their downstream judgements.
The answers require distinguishing the measured response patterns from claims
about underlying attitudes.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.5em]
\item For RQ1, the aggregate inventory score cannot be interpreted independently
of the model's general tendency to agree. Llama-90B scored $+0.153$, close
to the $0.160$ predicted by its mean agreement rate alone
(\cref{sec:s1-aggregate}). All five models agreed more readily with the
reverse-keyed items, although only Qwen-7B and Qwen-72B reached the
unadjusted significance threshold. The face experiments provided a separate
comparison: benevolent traits were attributed more readily to female
photographs in four models, with the opposite direction in Qwen-2B.
Four of the five gaps reached the corrected threshold, while Llama-11B
remained inconclusive (\cref{sec:s1b-gap}). These findings support reporting
item discrimination and the two subscales separately, alongside agreement
rates and response coverage.

\item For RQ2, the comparison did not establish that the human association
repeated across models. The correlation between intrinsic scores and signed
downstream deviations was $\rho=-0.70$, with $p=0.188$, across five models
from two families (\cref{sec:s2-summary}). Three models showed their most
negative deviation on ``smart person'', but ``warm person'' showed a less
consistent pattern (\cref{sec:s2-sigir}). This provides partial correspondence
on particular queries, without establishing that models with higher inventory
scores are less likely to recognise bias in search results.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Findings from logit-level auditing}\label{sec:disc-logit}

Reading probabilities allows the audit to distinguish limited response
coverage from an evenly divided preference between the permitted answers.
Both can produce similar aggregate scores, but they represent different
outcomes: one assigns probability to other continuations, while the other
balances the intended responses. Captured mass makes this distinction visible
in the Llama results (\cref{tab:capm}), although it cannot determine whether
the alternative continuation would be refusal, explanation or another format.

The robustness results reveal a different limitation concerning the stability
of ratings under equivalent scale definitions. The two largest models were
less robust in the image condition, but image presence and referent wording
changed together (\cref{sec:s2-anchor}). Identifying the contribution of each
requires a controlled comparison before attributing the difference to model
components. Arditi et al.\ \cite{arditi2024refusal} and Weng et al.\
\cite{weng2024images} provide examples of interventions and mediation analyses
that could inform such investigations, without establishing the mechanism in
these models.

The measured probabilities also need to be distinguished from generated
answers when considering implications for deployment. Wang et al.\
\cite{wang2024myanswer} reported disagreements between these outputs on
multiple-choice tasks, while Hu and Levy \cite{hu2023prompting} found
advantages for probability measurements in metalinguistic evaluation.
The present results describe responses at the measured token position;
evaluating unrestricted generated answers would require an additional study.

\section{Methodological lessons}\label{sec:disc-lessons}

The experience of implementing the audit suggests three practical requirements
for interpreting results from similar model evaluations.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.5em]
\item Measurement checks should precede comparisons between models or
experimental conditions. The six silent faults recorded in \cref{tab:faults}
produced plausible outputs, making successful execution and reasonable means
insufficient evidence of validity. Captured mass, output diversity and scoring
checks address different possible failures and should be recorded together.

\item Questionnaire scores should be examined for sensitivity to response
style before being interpreted as attitudes. The effects reported by Tjuatja
et al.\ \cite{tjuatja2024response} and Dominguez-Olmedo et al.\
\cite{dominguez2024questioning} reinforce this concern beyond the present
inventory. Here, the decomposition and polarity separation address uniform
shifts in agreement, while the permutation test retains its stated
exchangeability assumption.

\item Numerical checks should reflect the hardware configuration used for
inference, particularly when larger models require transfers between devices.
A successful run on one GPU does not validate execution across several GPUs.
The replication checks in \cref{sec:s1-replication} and the investigation in
\cref{app:fault} demonstrate the need to test numerical integrity under the
conditions used to obtain the reported measurements.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threats to validity}\label{sec:disc-threats}

Validity concerns the interpretation supported by a measurement, rather than
an inherent property of the instrument \cite{messick1995validity}.
\Cref{tab:threats} records the main limitations and the controls used to
address them.

Two differences are central to the interpretation of the human comparison.
Participants evaluated complete grids, whereas models rated individual images;
the baseline groups also use total ASI, while the original association
concerns benevolent sexism. A model rating below the baseline therefore
assigns lower objectivity, but is not necessarily less sexist than the
human group.

The adapted inventory and sampled photographs similarly bound the face
results, which measure trait attribution under the tested conditions.
Connecting these measurements to harms in deployment requires a separate
argument and evaluation \cite{blodgett2020language}. The statistical controls
do not remove these construct differences, enlarge the samples or establish
a general relationship between model size and bias.

\section{Ethics and responsible disclosure}\label{sec:disc-ethics}

The study used anonymised responses released by Otterbacher et al.\
\cite{otterbacher2018sigir}, without recruiting new human participants.
The Phase~1A controls were generated programmatically, while face images
were obtained from the Chicago Face Database \cite{ma2015cfd}.
The photographs remain outside the repository and are not reproduced in the
thesis, consistent with the database's restrictions on redistribution and
publication. The historical search stimuli are likewise presented through
measurements without reproducing photographs of their subjects.

The hardware fault documented in \cref{app:fault} was reported to UQ Research
Computing with a minimal reproduction, supporting investigation beyond the
affected audit runs. The findings are also reported with their uncertainty,
response coverage and sample limitations, so that measured differences remain
distinguishable from claims about underlying attitudes or behaviour in
systems that were not evaluated.
